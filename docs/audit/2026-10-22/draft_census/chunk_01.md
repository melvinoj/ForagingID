Used at a whisper, in a stable preserving medium, it's one of the most interesting condiments in a northern larder — and it keeps for months.<br><br>**Ingredients**<br><br>- 3 level tsp finely minced fresh wormwood leaves (weigh them; about 4 g — do not heap the spoon)<br>- 250 g clear honey<br>- 60 g unsalted butter<br>- 1 small shallot, minced very fine<br>- 1 tsp coarse sea salt<br>- 1 tbsp cider vinegar<br>- A few grinds of black pepper<br><br>**Method**<br><br>Pick the leaves young — before the plant flowers — from clean ground, away from roadsides and dogs. Rinse them cold, dry them properly in a cloth, and mince to a near-powder. Three level teaspoons is the whole batch. Don't be tempted to add "a bit more for character". Character is what lands people in a darkened room.<br><br>Warm the honey gently in a heavy pan — you want it fluid, not boiling; if it foams at the edge, pull it off the heat and let it settle. Stir in the minced leaves and leave it off the heat for ten minutes. What you're doing is an infusion, not a decoction: the heat pulls the aromatics and the bitterness out without driving the volatile oils off in steam. Taste it on the tip of a spoon after five minutes. You should get a bright, clean bitterness that fades reasonably quickly. If it clings and turns soapy, you've overdone the leaf — thin it with another spoon of honey and accept the lesson.<br><br>While that infuses, sweat the shallot in the butter over low heat until it's soft and sweet but not coloured, six or eight minutes. Add the salt and pepper. Now strain the honey through a fine sieve into the butter — press the leaves lightly, then discard them — and stir everything together over a very low flame until it's a smooth, glossy conserve. Take it off the heat, stir in the cider vinegar, and taste again. The vinegar isn't there to be noticed; it lifts the honey and keeps the whole thing from cloying. Adjust salt — a touch more than feels right is usually correct.<br><br>Pour into a clean jar, cool, and refrigerate. It will set firm as the butter chills. It keeps a fortnight in the fridge comfortably, longer if you're careful with the jar.<br><br>**To use**<br><br>A spoonful melted over roast roots — celeriac, parsnip, carrot — or folded through buttered barley. A small smear under the skin of a chicken before it goes in a hot oven. Stirred into a pan sauce for pork, with a splash of the roasting juices. The bitterness cuts fat and lengthens sweetness, which is exactly what you want from a conserve.<br><br>**One variation**<br><br>Swap half the honey for a good dark ale reduced down to a syrup, and add a pinch of crushed juniper. The hop bitterness and the wormwood bitterness sit side by side rather than fighting, and the juniper gives it a resinous edge that suits game and hard cheese. |
